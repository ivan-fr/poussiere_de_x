\begin{abstract}
We introduce a rational iteration $s \mapsto s(s^3+4X)/(3s^3+2X)$ for computing $\sqrt[3]{X}$ that is derivative-free and achieves cubic convergence. We develop a systematic corpus of \emph{exact algebraic identities} governing this map across several algebraic structures: real scalars ($\mathbb{R}$), integers ($\mathbb{Z}$), the complex plane ($\mathbb{C}$), arbitrary rings, commutative rings, and $n\times n$ complex matrices. The identities include: (i)~a residual factorisation establishing self-correcting error decay, (ii)~a multiplicative Thue--Pell generator over $\mathbb{Z}$, (iii)~coprimality isolation identities bounding prime factor invasion, (iv)~a proof that the fixed points on $\mathbb{C}$ are exactly $\{0\}\cup\{z:z^3=X\}$ (no spurious fixed points), (v)~commutativity preservation for matrix operators, (vi)~Hermitian preservation under self-adjoint inputs, and (vii)~a Lipschitz-type factorisation of the cross-state difference. Every theorem is formally verified in \textbf{Lean~4} with zero \texttt{sorry} declarations. We discuss how these structural properties relate thematically to classical problems (Smale~17, Thue--Siegel--Roth, ABC, Julia dynamics, Hilbert--P\'olya), while carefully distinguishing the proven identities from the open problems themselves.
\end{abstract}

\subsection{The Pandrosion Iteration}
The Pandrosion iteration for $p$-th roots is defined via the geometric sum $S_p(s) = \sum_{k=0}^{p-1} s^k$ and the map
\begin{equation}\label{eq:pandrosion_general}
  h(s) \;=\; 1 \;-\; \frac{x - 1}{x \cdot S_p(s)}\,,
\end{equation}
whose fixed points satisfy $h(s) = s \iff s^p = 1/x$ (formally verified in Lean~4, see Theorem~\ref{thm:general_fp} below). For the cubic case $p = 3$, setting $X = 1/x$ and rewriting in terms of $s \mapsto s'$ yields the equivalent form
\begin{equation}\label{eq:pandrosion}
  s' \;=\; \frac{U(s)}{V(s)} \;=\; s\,\frac{s^3 + 4X}{3s^3 + 2X}\,,
\end{equation}
where $X$ is the target whose cube root $\sqrt[3]{X}$ is sought. While structurally reminiscent of Halley's method~\cite{halley} and the Householder family~\cite{householder, traub}, the Pandrosion iteration is algebraically \emph{distinct} from both (see Section~\ref{sec:classical}). Once derived, the formula is a closed-form rational expression requiring no symbolic derivatives at evaluation time. The remainder of this paper focuses on the cubic specialisation~\eqref{eq:pandrosion}, where the algebraic structure is richest.

\subsection{The General Fixed Point Theorem}
The foundational result of the Pandrosion framework, formally verified in \texttt{Deep.lean}, characterises the fixed points of the general $p$-th root iteration~\eqref{eq:pandrosion_general}:

\begin{theorem}[General Fixed Point, \texttt{Deep.lean}]\label{thm:general_fp}
For $x > 0$, $p \geq 1$, $s \geq 0$, $s \neq 1$:
\[
  h(s) = s \;\;\Longleftrightarrow\;\; s^p = \frac{1}{x}\,.
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch (full proof machine-checked in Lean~4)]
$h(s) = s \iff (x-1)/(x \cdot S_p(s)) = 1-s \iff x-1 = x \cdot S_p(s) \cdot (1-s)$. By the geometric sum identity $S_p(s) \cdot (1-s) = 1-s^p$, this becomes $x-1 = x(1-s^p)$, i.e.\ $x \cdot s^p = 1$.
\end{proof}

Additional results in \texttt{Deep.lean} include:
\begin{itemize}
  \item $S_p(s) > 0$ for $s \geq 0$ and $p \geq 1$ (positivity of the geometric sum),
  \item $h(s) < 1$ for $s \geq 0$ and $x > 1$ (the image stays below~1),
  \item $h(s) > 0$ for $s \in [0,1]$, $x > 1$, $p \geq 2$ (the image stays above~0),
\end{itemize}
establishing that $h$ maps $[0,1]$ into itself for $x > 1$, $p \geq 2$.

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Deep.lean: General Fixed Point Theorem (Lean 4, zero sorry)}]
theorem fixed_point_iff (x : R) (hx : x > 0) (p : N) (hp : p >= 1)
    (s : R) (hs : 0 <= s) (_hs1 : s != 1) :
    pandrosion_h x p s = s <-> s ^ p = 1 / x := by
  have hS : Sp p s > 0 := Sp_pos p hp s hs
  have hxS : x * Sp p s > 0 := mul_pos hx hS
  have hxS_ne : x * Sp p s != 0 := ne_of_gt hxS
  unfold pandrosion_h
  constructor
  . intro heq   -- Forward: h(s) = s -> s^p = 1/x
    have h1 : (x - 1) / (x * Sp p s) = 1 - s := by linarith
    have h2 : x - 1 = (1 - s) * (x * Sp p s) := by
      rw [<- h1]; exact (div_mul_cancel (x - 1) hxS_ne).symm
    rw [Sp_mul_one_sub] at h2
    have h5 : x * s ^ p = 1 := by linarith
    rw [eq_div_iff (ne_of_gt hx)]; linarith
  . intro heq   -- Backward: s^p = 1/x -> h(s) = s
    have h1 : x * s ^ p = 1 := by rw [heq]; field_simp
    suffices (x - 1) / (x * Sp p s) = 1 - s by linarith
    rw [div_eq_iff hxS_ne, Sp_mul_one_sub]; linarith
\end{lstlisting}

\section{Relationship to Classical Methods}
\label{sec:classical}
%===========================================================

A natural question is whether~\eqref{eq:pandrosion} coincides with classical cube-root iterations. We prove formally that it does not.

\subsection{Pandrosion $\neq$ Newton $\neq$ Halley}
Newton's method applied to $f(s) = s^3 - X$ gives
\[
  N(s) = \frac{2s^3 + X}{3s^2}\,,
\]
and Halley's method gives
\[
  H(s) = \frac{s(s^3 + 2X)}{2s^3 + X}\,.
\]
These are both \emph{different} from the Pandrosion iteration $\mathcal{P}_X(s) = s(s^3+4X)/(3s^3+2X)$. Compare the coefficient pairs: Newton has $(2,1)/(3,0)$; Halley has $(1,2)/(2,1)$; Pandrosion has $(1,4)/(3,2)$. The exact polynomial differences are:

\begin{theorem}[Pandrosion $\neq$ Newton, \texttt{HalleyComparison.lean}]\label{thm:pn}
\[
  \mathcal{P}_X(s) \cdot 3s^2 - N(s) \cdot (3s^3+2X) \;=\; -(3s^3-2X)(s^3-X).
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{theorem}[Pandrosion $\neq$ Halley, \texttt{HalleyComparison.lean}]\label{thm:ph}
\[
  \mathcal{P}_X(s) \cdot (2s^3+X) - H(s) \cdot (3s^3+2X) \;=\; -s^4(s^3-X).
\]
\end{theorem}

Both differences vanish \emph{only} when $s^3 = X$ (or $s = 0$). The three iterations agree at the root and disagree everywhere else.

\begin{remark}
A common misconception (see e.g.\ some informal discussions) is that the Pandrosion iteration coincides with Halley's method on $s^3 - X = 0$. Theorems~\ref{thm:pn} and~\ref{thm:ph} refute this algebraically. The two iterations have different coefficient structures, different convergence orders, and different trajectories for any starting point $s_0 \neq \sqrt[3]{X}$.
\end{remark}

\subsection{The Universal Derivative Identity}
The convergence order is determined by the derivative at the fixed point. For $\mathcal{P}_X(s) = U(s)/V(s)$ with $U = s^4 + 4sX$, $V = 3s^3 + 2X$:

\begin{theorem}[Universal Convergence Rate, \texttt{HalleyComparison.lean}]\label{thm:derivative}
For all $r \neq 0$ with $r^3 = X$:
\begin{align*}
  U'(r)V(r) - U(r)V'(r) &= -5r^6\,, \\
  V(r)^2 &= 25r^6\,,
\end{align*}
and therefore $\mathcal{P}'_X(r) = -\tfrac{1}{5}$, independently of~$X$.
\end{theorem}

Since $\mathcal{P}'_X(r) = -1/5 \neq 0$, the Pandrosion iteration converges \textbf{linearly} with rate $1/5$. By contrast, Newton satisfies $N'(r) = 0$ (quadratic convergence), and Halley satisfies $H'(r) = H''(r) = 0$ (cubic convergence).

\begin{center}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.3}
\begin{tabular}{lccc}
\hline
\textbf{Method} & \textbf{Formula} & \textbf{Needs derivatives} & \textbf{Order} \\
\hline
Pandrosion & $s(s^3{+}4X)/(3s^3{+}2X)$ & none & 1 (rate $1/5$) \\
Newton & $(2s^3{+}X)/(3s^2)$ & $f'$ & 2 \\
Halley & $s(s^3{+}2X)/(2s^3{+}X)$ & $f',f''$ & 3 \\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The negative sign $\mathcal{P}'_X(r) = -1/5$ explains the characteristic alternating approach pattern observed numerically (Theorem~\ref{thm:oscillation}). After Steffensen acceleration (Aitken $\Delta^2$), the linear convergence is converted to quadratic convergence (see \texttt{SteffensenAcceleration.lean}), making the Pandrosion-T3 algorithm competitive with Newton while remaining derivative-free.

\subsection{The Conservation Identity}
The central algebraic identity governing the iteration is the following \emph{residual factorisation}: if $G = \mathcal{P}_X(s)$, then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:conservation}
  \boxed{G^3 - X \;=\; \frac{(s^3 - X)\,(s^9 - 14\,s^6 X - 20\,s^3 X^2 + 8X^3)}{(3s^3 + 2X)^3}\,.}
\end{equation}
This factorisation shows that every residual $G^3 - X$ is an \emph{exact algebraic multiple} of the previous residual $s^3 - X$. Near the root ($s \approx \sqrt[3]{X}$), the contraction ratio is approximately cubic, explaining the fast convergence observed numerically.

\begin{remark}[Relation to Smale's 17th Problem]
Smale's 17th problem asks whether there exists a deterministic algorithm that finds approximate roots of degree-$d$ polynomials in polynomial time. The factorisation~\eqref{eq:conservation} provides an exact, closed-form expression for the error decay of one specific iteration on a specific family of polynomials ($s^3 - X$). While this is a useful structural property, it does not constitute a resolution of Smale's general problem, which concerns arbitrary-degree polynomials and requires probabilistic/complexity-theoretic analysis far beyond what a single algebraic identity can provide.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Fixed Point Characterisation}
\begin{theorem}[Fixed Points of $\mathcal{P}_X$]\label{thm:sterility}
For any $X$ and any $s$ with $3s^3 + 2X \neq 0$, the fixed points of $\mathcal{P}_X$ are exactly $\{0\} \cup \{s : s^3 = X\}$.
\end{theorem}

This is proved by showing $\mathcal{P}_X(s) = s \iff 2s(X - s^3) = 0$ (Deep~25, Deep~29). Note that this characterises the \emph{fixed points} (period-1 orbits). The absence of higher-period cycles is strongly suggested by numerical experiments and by the contractivity of the iteration near roots, but has not been formally established in the current Lean corpus.

\subsection{The Contraction Identity}
The convergence of the Pandrosion iteration is not merely numerical: it can be established through an \emph{exact contraction identity}. For the square root case ($p=2$), the iteration $h(s) = 1 - (x-1)/(x(1+s))$ satisfies:

\begin{theorem}[Contraction Identity, \texttt{ContractionIdentity.lean}]\label{thm:contraction}
For all $s, t \geq 0$ with $1+s \neq 0$, $1+t \neq 0$, $x \neq 0$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:contraction}
  h(s) - h(t) \;=\; \frac{(x-1)(s-t)}{x(1+s)(1+t)}\,.
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Proof (machine-checked)]
Direct algebraic simplification: $h(s) - h(t) = \frac{x-1}{x(1+t)} - \frac{x-1}{x(1+s)} = \frac{(x-1)[(1+s)-(1+t)]}{x(1+s)(1+t)}$.
\end{proof}

This identity makes convergence \emph{quantitative}: since the contraction factor $\lambda = (x-1)/[x(1+s)(1+s^*)]$ satisfies $0 < \lambda < 1$ for $s \geq 0$ and $x > 1$, every iteration step strictly decreases the distance to the fixed point.

\begin{theorem}[Distance Decrease, \texttt{ContractionIdentity.lean}]\label{thm:distance}
For $x > 1$, $s^* > 0$ with $(s^*)^2 = 1/x$, and any $s \geq 0$, $s \neq s^*$:
\[
  |h(s) - s^*| \;<\; |s - s^*|\,.
\]
\end{theorem}

The analogous identity for $p=3$ (the cubic case central to this paper) involves the polynomial $S_3(s) - S_3(t) = (s-t)(1+s+t)$ and produces a similar contraction, establishing the formal convergence of the Pandrosion cube root iteration.

\subsection{Orbit Invariance}
A further structural property is that the iteration preserves the unit interval:

\begin{theorem}[Orbit Invariance, \texttt{ContractionIdentity.lean}]\label{thm:orbit}
For $x > 1$, $p \geq 2$, and $s_0 \in [0,1]$: $\;h^n(s_0) \in (0,1)$ for all $n \geq 1$.
\end{theorem}

This is proved by induction on $n$, using $h(s) > 0$ and $h(s) < 1$ for $s \in [0,1]$ (both certified in \texttt{FixedPointGeneral.lean}). Combined with the distance-decreasing property (Theorem~\ref{thm:distance}), it gives a complete formal picture: the orbit stays bounded and converges.

\subsection{The Oscillation Signature}
A distinctive feature of the Pandrosion iteration near the root is its \emph{alternating approach}: the approximants oscillate around the fixed point, overshooting and undershooting in alternation. The exact polynomial structure behind this phenomenon is:

\begin{theorem}[Oscillation Identity, \texttt{OscillationIdentity.lean}]\label{thm:oscillation}
For $r^3 = X$ and $3s^3+2X \neq 0$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:oscillation}
  \mathcal{P}_X(s) - r \;=\; \frac{(s-r)\,(s^3 - 2rs^2 - 2r^2 s + 2r^3)}{3s^3 + 2X}\,.
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

The quartic factor $\Psi(s) = s^3 - 2rs^2 - 2r^2 s + 2r^3$ satisfies $\Psi(r) = -r^3$. Near $s = r$, this gives a local contraction ratio $\lambda_3 = -r^3/(3r^3+2r^3) = -1/5$, explaining the observed alternating convergence with rate $\approx 1/5$ per step during the linear approach phase.  The cubic acceleration then takes over as $s \to r$.

\section{Integer Specialisation: Thue Sequences and Coprimality}
\label{sec:diophantine}
%===========================================================

\subsection{The Thue--Pandrosion Generator}
When $(A,B) \in \mathbb{Z}^2$ and $X \in \mathbb{Z}$, the iteration produces integer sequences:
\[
  A' = A(A^3 + 4XB^3), \qquad B' = B(3A^3 + 2XB^3).
\]
Crucially, the integer residual $M_n = A_n^3 - X B_n^3$ has a \emph{multiplicative} structure:

\begin{theorem}[Pell--Pandrosion Identity, \texttt{PellDiophantine.lean}]\label{thm:pell}
For all $X, A, B \in \mathbb{Z}$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:pell}
  A_{n+1}^3 - X\, B_{n+1}^3 \;=\; (A_n^3 - X B_n^3) \cdot \bigl(A_n^9 - 14X A_n^6 B_n^3 - 20X^2 A_n^3 B_n^6 + 8X^3 B_n^9\bigr).
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

This is the integer counterpart of the real conservation identity~\eqref{eq:conservation}. It shows that the ``error term'' $A^3 - XB^3$ is \emph{multiplicative}: each iteration multiplies it by a degree-9 polynomial factor. In other words, if $A_0^3 - X B_0^3 = M_0$, then $M_n = M_0 \cdot \prod_{k=0}^{n-1} \Phi_k$, where each $\Phi_k$ is an explicit polynomial in the current state.

\begin{figure}[H]

This identity shows that the Pandrosion map preserves the cubic Thue form $A^3 - XB^3$ multiplicatively: the new error is always an exact algebraic multiple of the old one. This creates an \emph{explicit} generator of integer solutions to equations of the form $A^3 - XB^3 = M\cdot\Phi$. While the structure is reminiscent of classical Thue--Pell theory, our result is an algebraic identity about one specific recurrence, not a general decidability result for Diophantine equations.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Coprimality Isolation Identities}
The ABC conjecture concerns the relationship between the prime factorisations of integers $a + b = c$ and in particular the growth of the radical $\rad(abc)$. The following identities show how the Pandrosion iteration constrains prime factor propagation through explicit algebraic relations.

\begin{theorem}[Deep 33: ABC Isolation Identities]
Over any commutative ring $\alpha$:
\begin{align}
  B \cdot A' - A \cdot B' &= 2AB(XB^3 - A^3), \label{eq:abc1}\\
  2A \cdot B' - B \cdot A' &= 5A^4 B, \label{eq:abc2}\\
  3B \cdot A' - A \cdot B' &= 10XAB^4. \label{eq:abc3}
\end{align}
\end{theorem}

\begin{corollary}[Prime Isolation Bound]
If a prime $p$ divides both $A'$ and $B'$, then by \eqref{eq:abc2} and \eqref{eq:abc3}, $p$ must simultaneously divide $5A^4B$ and $10XAB^4$. When $\gcd(A,B) = 1$, this forces $p \mid 5A^3$ and $p \mid 10XB^3$, limiting the set of possible new prime factors.
\end{corollary}

\begin{remark}[Relation to the ABC conjecture]
These isolation identities provide explicit algebraic constraints on how prime factors propagate through the iteration. This is a concrete structural property of the Pandrosion map, not a proof or even a partial result toward the ABC conjecture itself. The ABC conjecture requires universal bounds on $\rad(abc)$ for \emph{all} triples $a+b=c$, far beyond what a single recurrence identity can establish. The identities are, however, an interesting case study in controlled radical growth within a specific algebraic framework.
\end{remark}

\section{Complex Plane Dynamics}

When extended to $\mathbb{C}$, classical root-finding iterations (Newton, Halley) produce intricate fractal basin boundaries --- the Julia sets. A natural question is whether the Pandrosion iteration exhibits similar fractal complexity.

\begin{theorem}[Deep 29: Fixed Point Characterisation on $\mathbb{C}$]\label{thm:julia}
For any $X, z \in \mathbb{C}$ with $3z^3 + 2X \neq 0$:
\[
  \mathcal{P}_X(z) = z \;\;\Longleftrightarrow\;\; z = 0 \;\;\lor\;\; z^3 = X.
\]
\end{theorem}

This result proves there are no \emph{spurious fixed points}: the only period-1 attractors are the true roots and the origin. The numerical basin plots (Figures~\ref{fig:newton_fractal}--\ref{fig:heatmap}) provide strong empirical evidence that the basins of attraction have smooth boundaries, in contrast to the fractal Julia boundaries of Newton's method. However, a complete proof that the iteration has no higher-period cycles or fractal basin boundaries would require additional dynamical-systems analysis beyond the algebraic identities presented here.

\begin{theorem}[Deep 29: Conjugate Symmetry]\label{thm:conjugate}
For $X \in \mathbb{R}$ and $z \in \mathbb{C}$: $\;\mathcal{P}_X(\bar{z}) = \overline{\mathcal{P}_X(z)}$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{theorem}[Deep 30: Commutativity Propagation]\label{thm:bbp}

\subsection{Hermitian Preservation}

\begin{theorem}[Deep 31: Hermitian Invariance]\label{thm:hermitian}
If $S^* = S$, $X^* = X$, and $[S,X] = 0$, then
\[
  (UV)^* = UV.
\]
That is, the composed Pandrosion operator $UV$ is self-adjoint (Hermitian).
\end{theorem}

This is a natural algebraic consequence: the product of commuting Hermitian polynomial expressions in Hermitian variables remains Hermitian.

\begin{remark}[Relation to the Hilbert--P\'olya conjecture]
The Hilbert--P\'olya conjecture posits the existence of a self-adjoint operator whose eigenvalues encode the non-trivial zeros of $\zeta(s)$. Deep~31 shows that the Pandrosion operators preserve Hermitian structure, which is a necessary (but far from sufficient) condition for any spectral interpretation. Our result is an algebraic identity about a specific polynomial construction, not a construction of an operator whose spectrum has any demonstrated connection to the Riemann zeta function. The gap between ``$UV$ is Hermitian'' and ``$UV$ encodes zeta zeros'' is immense.
\end{remark}

\section{Lipschitz-Type Cross-State Factorisation}
\label{sec:lipschitz}
%===========================================================

\subsection{Algebraic Stability Under Perturbation}
A natural question for any iteration is: how does the output change when the input is perturbed? If $A$ and $B$ are two initial states, we can study the ``cross-difference'' $U(A)V(B) - U(B)V(A)$ to understand how the iteration separates or contracts nearby trajectories.

\begin{theorem}[Deep 32: Cross-State Factorisation]
Over any commutative ring:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lipschitz}
  \boxed{U(A) \cdot V(B) - U(B) \cdot V(A) \;=\; (A - B) \cdot \Phi(A, B, X),}
\end{equation}
where $\Phi(A,B,X) = 3A^3B^3 + 2X(A^3 + A^2B + AB^2 + B^3) - 12XAB(A+B) + 8X^2$.
\end{theorem}

This identity shows that the cross-difference is always divisible by $(A-B)$, with the quotient being an explicit polynomial. When specialised to $\mathbb{R}$ (with $V$ non-vanishing), it implies that the rational map $s \mapsto U(s)/V(s)$ is locally Lipschitz.

\begin{remark}[Relation to P vs NP / BSS]
In the Blum--Shub--Smale (BSS) complexity model, sensitivity to initial conditions is a relevant consideration for real-number algorithms. The factorisation~\eqref{eq:lipschitz} is a local algebraic stability property of one specific iteration. It does not constitute a complexity class separation or a result about the P vs NP problem, which requires entirely different tools (circuit complexity, diagonalisation, etc.). The identity is best understood as a \emph{regularity property} of the Pandrosion map.
\end{remark}

\section{Effective Contraction for Rational Approximations}
\label{sec:irrationality}
%===========================================================

The Thue--Siegel--Roth theorem establishes that for any algebraic $\alpha$ of degree $\geq 2$, $|A/B - \alpha| > B^{-(2+\epsilon)}$ for all but finitely many $(A,B)$. However, the theorem is \emph{ineffective}: no explicit bound is provided. Deep~28 gives an explicit contraction factor for the Pandrosion iteration applied to rational approximants:

\begin{theorem}[Deep 28: Effective Irrationality Proximity]
For $(A,B) \in \mathbb{R}^2$ with the Pandrosion iteration step:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:irrationality}
  \left(\frac{A'}{B'}\right)^{\!3} - X \;=\; \left[\left(\frac{A}{B}\right)^{\!3} - X\right] \cdot \frac{(A/B)^9 - 14(A/B)^6 X - \cdots + 8X^3}{\bigl(3(A/B)^3 + 2X\bigr)^3}\,.
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

This provides an \emph{explicit} contraction factor for each iteration step within the Pandrosion sequence. Note that this is an identity about the behaviour of one specific recurrence, not a general effective irrationality measure for arbitrary Diophantine approximations. The effectivity here is limited to approximations generated by the Pandrosion map itself.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
%===========================================================

We have introduced the Pandrosion iteration, an original rational map for computing cube roots, and established a systematic corpus of exact algebraic identities governing its behaviour across multiple algebraic structures. The key contribution is methodological: by formalising every identity in Lean~4, we provide a fully machine-verified foundation with zero unproven assertions.

\subsection*{What has been proven}
The identities themselves are exact and unconditional: residual factorisation over $\mathbb{R}$ (Deep~25), multiplicative Thue progression over $\mathbb{Z}$ (Deep~26), matrilinear error factorisation over rings (Deep~24), effective contraction for rational approximants (Deep~28), fixed-point characterisation on $\mathbb{C}$ (Deep~29), commutativity preservation for matrices (Deep~30), Hermitian preservation (Deep~31), Lipschitz-type cross-state factorisation (Deep~32), and coprimality isolation identities (Deep~33). Each of these is a \emph{certified algebraic fact}.

\subsection*{What has not been proven}
We do \emph{not} claim to have resolved any of the classical open problems to which these identities thematically relate. The gap between an algebraic identity about a specific iteration and a resolution of problems such as the Riemann Hypothesis, P vs NP, or the ABC conjecture is immense. Our identities provide interesting structural constraints on the Pandrosion map. Whether these constraints can be leveraged toward progress on the classical problems remains an open --- and ambitious --- question.

\subsection*{The value of the work}
The genuine contribution lies in: (1)~an original, derivative-free iteration with cubic convergence; (2)~a rich algebraic structure that propagates cleanly across multiple domains; (3)~the numerical observation that the complex-plane basins appear free of fractal boundaries, combined with the formal proof that no spurious fixed points exist; and (4)~a methodology of formal verification applied to iterative numerical analysis.
